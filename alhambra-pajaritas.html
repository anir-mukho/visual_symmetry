A wall in the Patio de los Arrayanes

Fourteenth-century tilework in the Alhambra, Granada. The craftsmen had no group theory and no notation for what they were doing.

It still has a symmetry group, and it is one of the seventeen. Which one?

We are going to find out from the photograph, not from a book.

Three questions settle it

  1. What are the translations? Find the two shortest ways of sliding the pattern onto itself. They generate the lattice, and its shape already rules most groups out.
  2. What is the highest order of rotation? Only 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 are possible — a theorem, not an observation. No pattern repeating in two directions has a five-fold centre.
  3. Is there a reflection, or a glide? This is the question people get wrong, because a pattern can look symmetric and have no mirror at all.

Answer those three and you have named one of the seventeen. Let us do it on the photograph above, measuring rather than guessing.

Step one — the translations

Slide the pattern until it lands on itself. The two shortest slides are drawn on the photograph; every dot is a point the whole pattern can be carried to.

Step two — the rotations

Turn the photograph about each marked point and see whether it lands on itself. Agreement between a pattern and its turned copy, measured pixel by pixel:

unrelated placement0.54
turn by 120°0.89
turn by 60°0.66
turn by 180°0.67

Three-fold, and no better. The three marked centres are not alike: 1 sits on a six-pointed star, 2 and 3 on the two different places where three birds come together.

Note what this costs the star. It is six-pointed, and it is not a six-fold centre of the pattern: the birds around it do not come back after 60°.

Three but not six leaves p3, p3m1, p31m.

Step three — is there a mirror?

the wall
the wall, reflected

Every bird curls the same way. Reflect, and every one of them curls the other way — and no amount of turning or sliding will put them back. The shape is chiral, so the pattern has no mirror. Searched over every axis direction, every offset and every glide, the best agreement was 0.79, below the 0.89 of the genuine three-fold turn. There is no reflection and no glide.

It is p3

Hexagonal lattice, three-fold rotation, no reflection, no glide. That is exactly one of the seventeen.

And a check we never put in: p3 says a cell must hold three inequivalent three-fold centres, mutually a distance a/√3 apart. Measured on the wall: 28.7 px, against a lattice step of 49.7. The ratio is 0.577; 1/√3 = 0.5774.

Here they are, dashed. They are three genuinely different places: a six-pointed star, and the two different points where three birds come together. The white hexagons, which look like they ought to be centres, are not — turning about one gives 0.62, no better than chance.

Two honest footnotes

Colour. We answered for the shapes alone. Put the colours back and most of those symmetries break, because the colouring here does not repeat with the pattern. Working shape-first is the standard move, and it is a choice, not a fact.

“All seventeen are in the Alhambra.” You will hear this. It has been argued over since 1944, when Edith Müller’s thesis found about a dozen. Grünbaum, Grünbaum and Shephard counted thirteen in 1986; Pérez-Gómez announced the missing four in 1987; Montesinos published photographs for all seventeen; Grünbaum disputed them again in 2006. Whether you count colour is part of what is being argued about.

The claim is folklore. The method is not.
